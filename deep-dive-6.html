Bucket 6

Collaborative Sensemaking & Distributed Cognition

Understanding can be built jointly when ideas are visible and taken up by others.

Why This Matters

When teachers say "group work is busy but shallow" or "one person does all the thinking," they're describing failures of collaborative sensemaking. Productive collaboration doesn't happen automatically—it requires structures that make thinking visible, shareable, and open to collective refinement.

Dialogic Learning

Understanding grows through the give-and-take of genuine dialogue.

Key Insight

Dialogic learning treats conversation not just as a vehicle for transmitting information, but as the space where understanding is constructed. In productive dialogue, ideas are articulated, questioned, elaborated, and revised. The quality of dialogue—not just its presence—determines whether collaboration leads to deeper understanding.

In Practice

  • Discussion consists of serial monologues rather than genuine exchange
  • Ideas are shared but not built upon or challenged
  • Questions are asked for compliance, not genuine inquiry

Design Implications

Teach dialogue moves explicitly (questioning, building, challenging). Use discussion structures that require uptake of others' ideas. Value reasoning over answers. Shift authority from teacher to reasoning.

Key References

Alexander (2008); Mercer (2000); Resnick, Michaels & O'Connor (2010)

Transactive Discussion

Learning happens when partners reason about each other's reasoning.

Key Insight

Transactive discussion involves operating on a partner's reasoning: questioning, extending, or critiquing their ideas rather than just stating your own. This mutual reasoning drives cognitive gains because it forces learners to consider perspectives and arguments they wouldn't generate alone.

In Practice

  • Group members take turns stating positions without engaging others' ideas
  • Disagreements are avoided rather than explored
  • Contributions are additive but not integrative

Design Implications

Structure tasks to require building on others' contributions. Assign roles that prompt questioning and elaboration. Create controversy worth resolving. Reward integration, not just participation.

Key References

Berkowitz & Gibbs (1983); Teasley (1995); Azmitia & Montgomery (1993)

Shared Representations

External representations make thinking visible for collective work.

Key Insight

When thinking is externalized—in diagrams, models, notations, or written text—it becomes a shared object that can be pointed at, revised, and reasoned about together. Shared representations create common ground and allow distributed cognition: thinking done across people and tools rather than in individual heads.

In Practice

  • Groups talk but don't create shared external artifacts
  • Representations are created by individuals and not jointly constructed
  • Ideas live in talk but aren't captured for inspection and revision

Design Implications

Provide shared workspaces and representational tools. Make joint construction of representations part of the task. Use visible artifacts as anchors for discussion. Design for revision, not just production.

Key References

Hutchins (1995); Roschelle (1992); Suthers (2006)

Accountable Talk

Discussion norms that hold contributions accountable to community, knowledge, and reasoning.

Key Insight

Accountable talk practices structure discussion so that participants are accountable to the learning community (listening and responding to others), to accurate knowledge (ensuring claims are grounded in evidence), and to rigorous reasoning (making thinking explicit and logical). These norms turn talk into a tool for learning.

In Practice

  • Contributions don't respond to what others have said
  • Claims are made without evidence or justification
  • Reasoning is implicit—only conclusions are shared

Design Implications

Establish and reinforce norms for productive talk. Use talk moves that prompt accountability. Model the kind of reasoning you want to see. Give feedback on discussion quality, not just content.

Key References

Michaels, O'Connor & Resnick (2008); Wolf, Crosson & Resnick (2005)

Participation Structures

How participation is organized shapes who thinks and how.

Key Insight

Participation structures—the rules, roles, and norms that govern who speaks, when, and about what—profoundly shape learning. Structures can concentrate thinking in few hands or distribute it widely. They can privilege certain voices or create equitable access. Changing participation structures changes what learning is possible.

In Practice

  • The same students always contribute; others are passive
  • Group work defaults to one person doing the thinking
  • Turn-taking is pro forma without real interdependence

Design Implications

Design roles that require interdependence. Use structures that ensure equitable participation. Create individual accountability within group tasks. Rotate roles to build diverse skills.

Key References

Cohen & Lotan (2014); Slavin (1995); Johnson & Johnson (1999)
