Attention, Working Memory & Cognitive Load

Learning is constrained by what learners attend to and can coordinate at once.

Why This Matters

When teachers say students are "overwhelmed" or "miss the important part," they're describing attention and working memory constraints. Our cognitive architecture has real limits—learning environments can work with these limits or against them.

Cognitive Load Theory

Working memory is limited; instruction should manage what fills it.

Key Insight

Working memory can hold only a few elements at once. Cognitive load comes from three sources: intrinsic (complexity of the material), extraneous (how instruction is designed), and germane (effort devoted to learning). When total load exceeds capacity, learning fails.

In Practice

  • Complex procedures are taught all at once instead of building up
  • Materials require mental integration that could be done by design
  • Novices are given expert-level problem-solving tasks too early

Design Implications

Reduce extraneous load through better design. Manage intrinsic load through sequencing and scaffolding. Use worked examples for novices. Physically integrate related information.

Key References

Sweller (1988); Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga (2011); Paas & van Merriënboer (1994)

Attention & Selection

We can't process everything; attention determines what enters the learning stream.

Key Insight

Attention is selective and limited. What learners attend to determines what they can learn. Attention can be guided by features of the environment (bottom-up) or by goals and expectations (top-down). Without appropriate attentional focus, even well-designed content is ineffective.

In Practice

  • Salient but irrelevant features capture attention (seductive details)
  • Important relationships are visually subtle and get missed
  • Learners don't know what to focus on in complex displays

Design Implications

Make important information visually salient. Remove or de-emphasize distracting elements. Provide explicit guidance about where to focus. Use signaling, cueing, and highlighting strategically.

Key References

Mayer & Moreno (2003); de Koning et al. (2009); Harp & Mayer (1998)

Multimedia Learning Principles

Words and pictures together work—but only when designed according to how minds work.

Key Insight

People have separate channels for processing visual and auditory information. Effective multimedia instruction uses both channels without overloading either. Poorly designed multimedia can create split attention, redundancy, or coherence problems that impede learning.

In Practice

  • Text is placed far from the images it describes (split attention)
  • Same information presented in both text and narration simultaneously
  • Interesting but irrelevant content added to make materials engaging

Design Implications

Present words as narration with graphics, not on-screen text. Place text near corresponding graphics. Eliminate extraneous content. Segment complex presentations into learner-paced chunks.

Key References

Mayer (2009); Clark & Mayer (2016); Moreno & Mayer (1999)

Prior Knowledge Activation

What learners already know changes what they can attend to and hold in mind.

Key Insight

Prior knowledge dramatically affects working memory demands. Experts can chunk information into meaningful units, reducing load. Without activated prior knowledge, learners must hold more individual elements in working memory. The "expertise reversal effect" means techniques that help novices can hinder experts.

In Practice

  • Instruction assumes prior knowledge that hasn't been activated
  • Scaffolds designed for novices frustrate more advanced learners
  • Relevant prior knowledge exists but isn't connected to new content

Design Implications

Activate relevant prior knowledge before introducing new content. Assess what learners already know. Adjust scaffolding based on expertise level. Help learners see connections to what they know.

Key References

Kalyuga et al. (2003); Kalyuga (2007); Bransford & Johnson (1972)

Desirable Difficulties

Some cognitive challenges enhance learning even though they feel harder.

Key Insight

Not all cognitive load is bad. "Desirable difficulties" are challenges that slow initial learning but enhance long-term retention and transfer. The key is distinguishing between difficulties that promote deep processing versus those that simply waste cognitive resources.

In Practice

  • Spacing practice over time feels harder but produces better retention
  • Interleaving problem types is more confusing but improves discrimination
  • Generation and retrieval practice are harder than re-reading but more effective

Design Implications

Distinguish productive struggle from unproductive confusion. Make learning feel appropriately challenging. Don't optimize for immediate performance at the expense of long-term learning.

Key References

Bjork (1994); Bjork & Bjork (2011); Roediger & Karpicke (2006)
