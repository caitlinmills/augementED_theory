Deep Dive #1

Mental Representations & Knowledge Networks

How to use this page: This is a mechanism-first reference. Each concept follows: Mechanism → How it shows up → What change requires → Example interventions → Cautions & limits. Scan vertically to find what fits the story you're hearing.

1. Knowledge Organization & Model Building

Understanding depends on how ideas are connected, not how many are known. Comprehension is the active construction of coherent mental models.

The Mechanism

Two complementary frameworks ground this concept. Kintsch's Construction-Integration (CI) model distinguishes the textbase (a surface encoding of what was explicitly stated) from the situation model (an integrated representation of what the content means, connected to prior knowledge). Comprehension fails when learners build an adequate textbase but never construct a coherent situation model—they can repeat information without understanding it.

Expertise research (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981) shows that experts and novices differ primarily in how their knowledge is organized, not how much they know. Novice networks are sparse and organized around surface features ("problems with pulleys"). Expert networks are dense, hierarchical, and organized around deep principles ("conservation of energy problems").

The mechanistic link: comprehension requires activating relevant prior knowledge and integrating new information through a constraint-satisfaction process that strengthens coherent connections. When prior knowledge is weak, poorly organized, or doesn't connect to the new material, integration fails. Retrieval and reasoning depend on spreading activation—in sparse or disorganized networks, relevant knowledge doesn't come online when needed.

How It Shows Up

  • Students can recall or recite information but can't explain why it matters or how pieces connect
  • Fluent verbal performance collapses under probing questions
  • Students 'get lost' mid-task because they're tracking steps, not meaning
  • Minor surface changes to a problem (different numbers, different context) derail performance
  • Students sort problems by surface features ('this one has a ramp') rather than deep structure
  • Experts are puzzled why students 'can't see' an obvious connection

What Change Requires

Tasks must require situation-model-level processing, not just textbase recall. Learners need prompts that force active integration—connecting new information to prior knowledge, not just encoding it. Instruction should surface and address gaps or misorganization in prior knowledge before trying to build on it. Progress should be measured in terms of connections and organization, not just correctness on isolated items.

Example Interventions

  • Self-explanation prompts (Chi et al., 1994): Students explain each step of a worked example to themselves, forcing them to connect new information to what they already know.
  • Coherence-building questions mid-task: Teachers pause and ask 'How does this connect to what we established earlier?' before students proceed.
  • Concept mapping revisited over time: Students build and revise visual representations of how ideas relate as new content is introduced.
  • Problem-sorting tasks (Chi et al.): Students categorize problems and discuss the basis for their categories, making organizational structure visible.
  • Assessments targeting relationships: Ask 'How does X relate to Y?' or 'When would you use X vs. Y?' rather than 'What is X?'

Cautions & Limits

When this lens doesn't apply:

  • When learners already have coherent, well-organized knowledge but disengage for motivational or affective reasons (see Bucket 5)
  • When the issue is retrieval failure (knowledge exists but isn't accessed in context) rather than representation (see Transfer section below)

Ways this could go wrong:

  • Pushing for integration before students have sufficient pieces to integrate—you can't connect what isn't there
  • Assuming 'more connections = better' without attending to whether the connections are accurate
  • Over-scaffolding integration so students never learn to do it independently

Ethical considerations:

  • Whose prior knowledge? Educational materials often assume particular cultural contexts, examples, and experiences. Students whose lived experiences match the materials get to integrate; students whose don't face a double burden.
  • Labeling students as having 'fragmented' or 'shallow' knowledge can slip into deficit framing. The same knowledge organization that looks 'novice-like' in school may be expert-like in other contexts.

2. Knowledge-in-Pieces: Fragmentation as a Developmental Stage

Inconsistency is often a normal feature of learning in progress, not a failure to be corrected.

The Mechanism

diSessa's "knowledge-in-pieces" framework argues that intuitive knowledge consists of many small, context-dependent elements—phenomenological primitives or "p-prims"—rather than coherent theories. These are locally useful intuitions that get activated in specific situations.

Learning involves gradual coordination and reorganization of these pieces, not wholesale replacement of "misconceptions." This reframes inconsistency: when a student reasons differently about structurally similar problems, it doesn't mean they "don't get it"—it means their pieces aren't yet coordinated. Conceptual change is gradual alignment and systematization, not sudden insight.

The mechanism: p-prims are cued by context, so different surface features activate different intuitions. Coherence develops as learners notice conflicts between their intuitions and gradually systematize them into more principled knowledge. This takes time and requires encountering the same deep structure in varied contexts.

How It Shows Up

  • Students contradict themselves across examples without noticing
  • The same student gives different explanations for structurally identical problems in different contexts
  • 'Almost right' reasoning that regresses under pressure or in unfamiliar formats
  • Progress looks non-monotonic—students seem to 'get worse' before getting better
  • Partial explanations that are locally sensible but globally inconsistent

What Change Requires

Instruction must allow time and space for reconciliation rather than rushing toward coherence. Learners need opportunities to encounter the same deep structure in varied surface forms so their inconsistent intuitions come into contact. Contradictions should be surfaced productively, not suppressed. Assessment should expect and track inconsistency as a developmental stage, not just penalize it.

Example Interventions

  • Contrasting cases (Schwartz & Bransford): Students see two cases side by side that share deep structure but differ on surface (or vice versa) and must identify what's the same or different.
  • Bridging analogies (Clement, 1993): A sequence of intermediate cases that gradually bridge from an anchoring intuition (where students reason correctly) to the target case (where they typically don't).
  • Predict-observe-explain sequences: Students commit to a prediction, observe what happens, and must reconcile the gap—surfacing the conflict between intuitions.
  • Delaying correction: Instead of immediately fixing errors, teachers surface competing ideas and let students sit with the tension before resolving.

Cautions & Limits

When this lens doesn't apply:

  • When misconceptions are stable, resistant, and theory-like rather than fragmented—some wrong ideas are entrenched, not just uncoordinated
  • When the issue is missing knowledge entirely, not uncoordinated pieces

Ways this could go wrong:

  • Letting students sit in confusion for too long without support, which can undermine confidence and motivation
  • Treating all inconsistency as productive when sometimes it reflects genuine confusion that needs direct instruction
  • Assuming students will spontaneously notice and reconcile contradictions—most need structured support

Ethical considerations:

  • Publicly surfacing students' contradictions can be embarrassing or threatening, especially for students already feeling marginalized. How contradictions are surfaced matters—private reflection vs. public exposure.
  • The framing of 'fragmented' knowledge can feel like a judgment. Students may have coherent knowledge that's simply organized around different principles than school expects.

3. Representational Alignment: External Forms Shape Internal Models

The diagrams, symbols, and language we use don't just express understanding—they shape what understanding gets built.

The Mechanism

Ainsworth's DeFT (Design, Functions, Tasks) framework analyzes how multiple external representations support learning. Key insight: external representations are not neutral windows onto content. They highlight certain relations and obscure others. A graph emphasizes different features than an equation, which emphasizes different features than a verbal description.

Learners must learn to "read" representations, understand what each one shows and hides, and translate between them. Representational alignment refers to the match between the structure emphasized by an external representation and the conceptual structure learners need to build.

Misalignment creates illusions of understanding: students can manipulate symbols correctly (solving equations, completing diagrams) without grasping what the symbols mean. Conversely, well-aligned representations scaffold construction of appropriate mental models by making the right relations salient.

How It Shows Up

  • Procedural success without conceptual understanding—students solve equations but can't explain what the variables mean
  • Students copy diagrams but don't use them when problem-solving
  • Confusion or errors when switching between representations (graph to equation, diagram to verbal)
  • Decorative representations that don't connect to the core ideas
  • Students who can manipulate a representation but can't interpret it or explain what it shows

What Change Requires

Representations must be chosen deliberately to align with target conceptual structure—not just to look nice or because they're conventional. Learners need explicit support to translate between representations and understand what each one affords. Instruction should treat representations as tools with strengths and limits, not transparent windows onto truth.

Example Interventions

  • Translation tasks: Students express the same idea in multiple representations—diagram, equation, verbal—and explain how each connects to the others.
  • Representation analysis prompts: 'What does this diagram show? What does it hide? What couldn't you figure out from this alone?'
  • Stripping seductive details (Harp & Mayer): Removing interesting but irrelevant illustrations that capture attention without supporting understanding.
  • Progressive formalization: Starting with intuitive representations close to the phenomenon, then gradually introducing more abstract/formal representations as understanding develops.

Cautions & Limits

When this lens doesn't apply:

  • When students understand the representations fine but lack motivation or self-regulation to use them
  • When the issue is missing domain knowledge, not representational competence

Ways this could go wrong:

  • Overwhelming students with multiple representations before they've mastered any one
  • Assuming translation between representations is easy or automatic—it's a skill that needs to be taught
  • Removing 'seductive details' that actually do serve motivational or cultural purposes for some learners

Ethical considerations:

  • Whose representations count as 'standard' or 'clear'? Diagramming conventions, mathematical notations, and even what counts as a 'good' explanation are culturally situated. Students from different backgrounds may find different representations intuitive.
  • Privileging formal/abstract representations over intuitive/concrete ones can devalue the knowledge students bring.

4. Transfer: Recognizing Deep Similarity Across Contexts

Transfer depends on encoding and retrieving structure—not memorizing answers.

The Mechanism

Gentner's structure-mapping theory explains analogical reasoning: learners must extract relational structure from a source case and map it onto a target. Transfer fails when learners encode cases in terms of surface features (objects, contexts, cover stories) rather than relational structure (how elements interact, what causes what).

This explains "inert knowledge"—students learn something in one context but fail to apply it elsewhere. The knowledge exists, but retrieval is cued by surface features that don't match the new situation. The deep structural similarity isn't noticed.

Transfer thus requires two things: (1) encoding structure during initial learning, not just surface features, and (2) recognizing structural alignment at retrieval, even when surfaces differ. Both are hard and neither is automatic.

How It Shows Up

  • 'They already learned this!' failures—teachers know students have the relevant knowledge but students don't access it
  • Strong performance on practiced problems, collapse on transfer assessments or novel contexts
  • Learning in one class or unit doesn't transfer to another
  • Students don't spontaneously use relevant prior knowledge unless explicitly cued
  • Context-bound competence that doesn't travel

What Change Requires

Initial learning must emphasize relational structure, not just procedures or facts. Practice should vary surface features while preserving deep structure, so learners extract what's invariant. Instruction should explicitly support retrieval cues based on structure ("This is like the X problem—what's the same?"), not just context.

Example Interventions

  • Comparison of solved examples (Rittle-Johnson & Star): Students compare two worked solutions side by side and identify similarities/differences. Cases are chosen to highlight structural features.
  • Varied practice across surface contexts: Instead of massed practice on identical problems, learners encounter the same principle in multiple surface forms.
  • Explicit structural cueing at retrieval: 'This is like the [anchor problem] we solved before—what's the same? What's different?'
  • Analogical encoding (Gentner): Comparing two analogous cases during learning, which promotes extraction of shared structure.

Cautions & Limits

When this lens doesn't apply:

  • When basic comprehension hasn't formed yet—you can't transfer what you don't understand
  • When the knowledge is there and structurally encoded, but affective factors (anxiety, identity threat) block access

Ways this could go wrong:

  • Varying surfaces too early, before students have a solid grasp of even one instantiation
  • Assuming structural similarity is obvious—it's often only obvious to experts who already see the structure
  • Teaching for transfer without ensuring initial learning is solid

Ethical considerations:

  • What counts as 'deep structure' vs. 'surface feature' isn't always neutral. Features that seem superficial in school contexts may carry real meaning in students' lives and communities.
  • Transfer expectations can be inequitable when they assume students have encountered the relevant structures in prior schooling—students with interrupted education may have the capacity but lack the specific structural exposure.

Cross-Bucket Connections

  • Representation issues often co-occur with inference problems (Bucket 3): students may have organized knowledge but not generate inferences from it.
  • When students have the knowledge but don't use it, consider monitoring/regulation (Bucket 4) or epistemic stance/affect (Bucket 5).
  • If individual understanding is fine but group work isn't productive, see collaborative sensemaking (Bucket 6).
